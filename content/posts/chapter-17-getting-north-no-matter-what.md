the elk in the distance was the coolest thing ever, I spent the morning walking up on herds of elk and trying to sneak closer for a view.&nbsp; I guess no matter how old or large I get, something about the dark will always spook me.&nbsp; (I wonder if at 80 I will still have to fight not to run up the stairs after turning off basement lights?)</p><p>Anyways, after hiking in the morning cold and rain, the sun came out for a half hour and gave me some beautiful blue sky.&nbsp; However, less than 30 minutes after that blue sky I was hailed on (and not as emperor).&nbsp; After some time hiking through that wintry mix I heard a lightning strike somewhat close to me on the mountain.&nbsp; Walking above tree line holding aluminum hiking poles made me a prime target and so I jogged the next half mile to below tree line&#8230;&nbsp;</p><p>-I confess there is a part of me that is very confident I would survive a lightning strike (and a smaller but real part that hoped for superpowers) But nonetheless I went down to safety.&nbsp;</p><p>Less than an hour after that it cleared up enough for views of the majestic Mt. Rainier.&nbsp; Most PCT hikers refuse to call it Rainier and instead call it Tahoma or &#8220;Mother Mountain/Mother of Mountains&#8221; as the native Americans did.&nbsp; As you may expect, normally I&#8217;m not one for that sort of thing&#8211;in this case I am surprisingly sympathetic. Rainier is so spectacular and is so terrifyingly motherly that it deserves that special reverence.&nbsp; It is a truly powerful mountain.&nbsp; Out of admiration, I settled on a very cold/wet campsite
